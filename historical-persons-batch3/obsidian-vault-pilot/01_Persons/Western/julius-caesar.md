# Gaius Julius Caesar / 盖乌斯·尤利乌斯·凯撒

## Summary

Roman general, statesman, and dictator. Conqueror of Gaul (58–50 BCE), victor in the Civil War against Pompey (49–45 BCE), and dictator perpetuo until his assassination in 44 BCE. Author of *Commentarii de Bello Gallico* and *de Bello Civili*. His name became the imperial title "Caesar."

罗马将军、政治家和独裁官。征服高卢（前58–50年），内战中击败庞培（前49–45年），任终身独裁官至前44年被刺杀。著有《高卢战记》和《内战记》。其名号成为后世罗马皇帝头衔。

## Key Historical/Reception/Fiction Separation

### Confirmed Historical Facts
- Conquest of Gaul (58–50 BCE)
- Crossing the Rubicon (49 BCE) — act of insurrection triggering civil war
- Victory over Pompey at Pharsalus (48 BCE)
- Appointment as dictator perpetuo (February 44 BCE)
- Assassination on the Ides of March (15 March 44 BCE)
- Authorship of *Commentarii de Bello Gallico* and *de Bello Civili*
- Calendar reform establishing the Julian calendar

### Source Caveats
- Caesar's own *Commentarii*: primary source but self-promotional — written for a Roman political audience
- Plutarch's *Life of Caesar*: written over a century later; draws on earlier sources now lost
- Suetonius' *Divus Julius*: includes biographical detail alongside anecdotal and possibly embellished material
- Cicero's letters: contemporary but from a political rival
- Cassius Dio: later synthesis (3rd century CE)

### Reception / Historical Layering
- "First Triumvirate": An informal political arrangement, not a formal institution. The term was applied by later historians.
- Imperial title "Caesar": His name was adopted as a title by Roman emperors (and later became Kaiser, Tsar) — a posthumous development, not his intent.
- "Crossing the Rubicon": Became a metaphor for irreversible decisions — this cultural layering postdates the historical event.

### Literary / Fictional
- "Et tu, Brute?": Literary invention — first appears in Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* (1599), not in ancient sources. Suetonius reports Caesar spoke Greek (καὶ σύ, τέκνον) or said nothing.
- Caesar salad: Modern culinary invention (Caesar Cardini, 1920s, Tijuana) — no connection to Roman history.
- Caesarean section etymology: Folk etymology — the term likely derives from Latin *caedere* (to cut), not from Caesar's birth.
