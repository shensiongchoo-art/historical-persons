# Marcus Junius Brutus — Biographical Profile

## Identity and Family

Marcus Junius Brutus (c. 85–42 BCE) was a Roman senator and politician of the late Republic. He was born into two of Rome's most distinguished families: his father's line, the Junii Bruti, claimed descent from Lucius Junius Brutus, the legendary consul who overthrew the Roman monarchy in 509 BCE. His mother Servilia was from the Servilii Caepiones and was connected to the Porcii Catones. Servilia was also Julius Caesar's longtime mistress — a relationship that would cast a long shadow over Brutus's life.

Brutus was formally adopted by his uncle Quintus Servilius Caepio, taking the name Quintus Caepio Brutus. He married Porcia, daughter of Marcus Porcius Cato Uticensis (Cato the Younger), his uncle by marriage.

## Intellectual Life

Before his political notoriety, Brutus was known as one of the finest Latin intellectuals of his generation. Cicero dedicated several major philosophical works to him — the *Brutus* (a history of Roman oratory), the *Orator*, *De Finibus*, *Tusculan Disputations*, and *De Natura Deorum* — and featured him as an interlocutor. Brutus himself wrote philosophical treatises on virtue, duty, and endurance, all now lost except for fragments. His intellectual reputation was substantial and independent of his later political role.

## Civil War and Caesar's Pardon

When civil war broke out in 49 BCE, Brutus faced a difficult choice. His mother was Caesar's mistress, and Caesar had shown him personal favor. But his uncle Cato was the most prominent anti-Caesarian, and the Junii Bruti tradition was anti-tyranny. Brutus chose to side with Pompey and the senatorial cause. After Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus (48 BCE), Brutus surrendered and was pardoned by Caesar, who not only spared him but promoted him — governor of Cisalpine Gaul in 46 BCE, and the prestigious office of urban praetor for 44 BCE.

## The Assassination

The relationship between Caesar's favors and Brutus's eventual decision to assassinate him is the central puzzle of his life. According to Plutarch, Gaius Cassius Longinus (Brutus's brother-in-law) first proposed assassinating Caesar in early 44 BCE. Brutus was persuaded to join and became the conspiracy's moral center. On 15 March 44 BCE, approximately sixty senators stabbed Caesar to death in the Senate chamber at the Theater of Pompey. Brutus was among the principal assailants.

What Caesar said when he saw Brutus among the attackers is uncertain. Suetonius reports a Greek phrase, but other sources say Caesar was silent. The famous "Et tu, Brute?" is Shakespeare's invention (1599) and has no basis in ancient sources.

## Aftermath and Philippi

The conspirators failed to control the political aftermath. Mark Antony seized Caesar's papers and treasury, delivered the inflammatory funeral oration, and rapidly outmaneuvered Brutus and his allies. By April 44 BCE, Brutus had left Rome. He and Cassius raised armies in the eastern provinces — Brutus took Macedonia, Cassius took Syria — preparing for war against the Caesarian faction.

In November 43 BCE, Octavian, Antony, and Lepidus formed the legally ratified Second Triumvirate and initiated proscriptions. Brutus and Cassius were named as public enemies. Their forces met the triumviral armies at Philippi in Macedonia in October 42 BCE. In the first engagement, Brutus repulsed Octavian's forces, but Cassius was simultaneously defeated by Antony and committed suicide. Three weeks later, in the second engagement, Antony's forces broke Brutus's lines. Brutus fled and committed suicide, reportedly saying that virtue was only a word.

## Reception and Legend

No figure from Roman history has been more contested in later reception than Brutus. Dante placed him in the lowest circle of Hell alongside Judas and Cassius. Shakespeare made him a tragic hero. The French Revolution celebrated him as a republican martyr. The U.S. Founders invoked his name. These are reception traditions, not historical evidence. The historical Brutus is accessible primarily through Plutarch's biography (written ~150 years after his death), Cicero's letters and dialogues, and the historical narratives of Appian and Cassius Dio — all of which reflect their own biases and literary purposes.

Modern scholarship (Tempest 2017, Rawson 1986) emphasizes the complexity of Brutus's motives and the political calculations behind the assassination. The popular image of Brutus as a purely noble, selfless tyrannicide is a literary construct. His own EID MAR coinage — celebrating the assassination on the coin of the realm — suggests a man acutely conscious of his public image and political messaging.
