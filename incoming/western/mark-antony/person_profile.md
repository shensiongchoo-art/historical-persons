# Mark Antony — Biographical Profile

## Overview

Mark Antony (Marcus Antonius, 83–30 BCE) was a Roman politician and general, one of the most prominent figures of the late Roman Republic. A trusted lieutenant of Julius Caesar, Antony rose to power after Caesar's assassination in 44 BCE, formed the Second Triumvirate with Octavian (the future Augustus) and Lepidus in 43 BCE, and ruled the Roman East from 42 to 30 BCE. His political and romantic alliance with Cleopatra VII of Egypt led to a final confrontation with Octavian, culminating in his defeat at the naval Battle of Actium (31 BCE) and his suicide in Alexandria (30 BCE).

## Early Life and Rise under Caesar (83–48 BCE)

Antony was born on 14 January 83 BCE into the plebeian gens Antonia. His father, Marcus Antonius Creticus, died young and in disgrace after a failed campaign against Cretan pirates. His mother, Julia Antonia, was a cousin of Julius Caesar. Antony's early military career began under Aulus Gabinius in Syria and Egypt (57–55 BCE), where he distinguished himself as a cavalry commander.

In 54 BCE, Antony joined Caesar in Gaul, serving as a military officer during the late Gallic campaigns. He returned to Rome and was elected Tribune of the Plebs for 49 BCE. When the Senate ignored his tribunician veto, Antony fled to Caesar at Ravenna — the event Caesar cited as justification for crossing the Rubicon. During the subsequent civil war, Antony commanded Caesar's left wing at the decisive Battle of Pharsalus (48 BCE) and was appointed Magister Equitum (Master of Horse), effectively governing Italy while Caesar campaigned in the East.

## Caesar's Heir Apparent (48–44 BCE)

After Caesar's return from Egypt, Antony became his most trusted deputy. Caesar chose Antony as his consular colleague for 44 BCE. At the Lupercalia festival on 15 February 44 BCE, Antony publicly offered Caesar a royal diadem three times — an event that alarmed republican traditionalists. On 15 March 44 BCE, Caesar was assassinated by a group of senators led by Brutus and Cassius. Antony was detained outside the Senate chamber and escaped.

In the aftermath, Antony initially negotiated a compromise with the conspirators. However, his dramatic funeral oration — in which he read Caesar's will and displayed the blood-stained toga — incited the Roman crowd against the assassins, who fled the city. Antony seized Caesar's papers and treasury, positioning himself as Caesar's political successor. This brought him into conflict with Cicero, who delivered the fourteen *Philippics* denouncing Antony as a tyrant, and with Caesar's young heir, Octavian.

## The Second Triumvirate (43–33 BCE)

After being declared a public enemy and defeated at Mutina (43 BCE), Antony retreated to Gaul and joined forces with Lepidus. In November 43 BCE, Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate — a legally constituted board of three supreme magistrates empowered to "restore the Republic." They published proscription lists; an estimated 300 senators and 2,000 equites were executed, most notably Cicero, whose head and hands Antony ordered displayed on the Rostra.

The triumvirs then marched east to confront Caesar's assassins. At the two battles of Philippi (October 42 BCE), Antony played the leading military role in defeating Brutus and Cassius, both of whom committed suicide. In the division of the empire that followed, Antony took the wealthy eastern provinces.

## Cleopatra and the East (41–34 BCE)

In 41 BCE, Antony summoned Cleopatra VII to Tarsus to answer for her alleged support of Cassius. Cleopatra arrived in spectacular fashion, and Antony was captivated. He spent the winter of 41–40 BCE with her in Alexandria; their twins Alexander Helios and Cleopatra Selene were born in 40 BCE.

Tensions with Octavian erupted when Antony's brother Lucius Antonius and wife Fulvia waged the Perusine War against Octavian in Italy (41–40 BCE). After Fulvia's death, Antony and Octavian reconciled at the Pact of Brundisium (40 BCE), sealed by Antony's marriage to Octavia, Octavian's sister.

Despite this marriage — which produced two daughters — Antony resumed his relationship with Cleopatra. His Parthian campaign of 36 BCE was a military disaster, but he recovered prestige by capturing Armenia in 34 BCE. That same year, Antony staged the "Donations of Alexandria," a grand ceremony in which he proclaimed Cleopatra "Queen of Kings" and assigned Roman eastern territories to her children. Octavian used this as devastating propaganda in Rome.

## Final Conflict and Death (32–30 BCE)

In 32 BCE, Antony formally divorced Octavia. Octavian obtained what he claimed was Antony's will from the Vestal Virgins and read its contents to the Senate — including provisions confirming the Donations of Alexandria and a wish to be buried in Alexandria. The Senate stripped Antony of his powers and declared war on Cleopatra.

The decisive confrontation came at the naval Battle of Actium on 2 September 31 BCE, where Octavian's admiral Agrippa trapped and defeated Antony and Cleopatra's fleet. They fled to Alexandria. Antony's land legions surrendered a week later. In August 30 BCE, Octavian's forces entered Alexandria. Believing Cleopatra already dead, Antony stabbed himself with his sword. He was carried to her mausoleum and died in her arms, according to Plutarch. Cleopatra followed in suicide on 10 August.

## The Augustan Propaganda Problem

The surviving ancient sources on Antony — Plutarch, Appian, Cassius Dio, and even Cicero — are all written from the perspective of his enemies or heavily influenced by Augustan propaganda. Octavian's victory was total, and the narrative of Antony as a once-great Roman corrupted by eastern decadence and a foreign queen became canonical. Modern historians (Syme, Pelling, Goldsworthy) emphasize the need to read these sources critically and distinguish historical Antony from the propaganda figure.

## Legacy

Antony's defeat and death removed the last major obstacle to Octavian's sole power. Three years later, in 27 BCE, Octavian became Augustus, the first Roman emperor. Antony's descendants through Octavia (Antonia Major and Minor) became part of the Julio-Claudian dynasty: Antonia Minor was the mother of the emperor Claudius and grandmother of Caligula. Antony's daughter with Cleopatra, Cleopatra Selene II, became queen of Mauretania.
