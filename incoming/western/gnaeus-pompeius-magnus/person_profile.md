# Pompey (Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus, 106–48 BCE)

## Identity

Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (29 September 106 BCE – 28 September 48 BCE), known in English as Pompey or Pompey the Great, was a military and political leader of the late Roman Republic. He was born in Picenum (modern Marche/Abruzzo, Italy) to Gnaeus Pompeius Strabo, consul of 89 BCE and the first of the Pompeii to reach senatorial status.

Pompey's early career was meteoric. At age 23, as a private citizen, he raised and equipped a full legion from his father's former clients and veterans in Picenum to support Sulla in the civil war of 83 BCE. Sulla greeted him with the title *imperator*. Over the next two years, Pompey defeated Marian forces in Sicily and Africa, earning a triumph in 81 BCE at approximately age 25 — unprecedented for someone so young who had not yet held public office. His troops, comparing him to Alexander the Great, began calling him *Magnus* ("the Great"), a cognomen he later formally adopted. His enemies coined a different nickname: *adulescentulus carnifex* — "teenage butcher" — for his ruthless executions of Marian supporters.

## Major Military Campaigns

### Sertorian War (76–71 BCE)

Pompey was sent to Hispania with proconsular authority against Quintus Sertorius, a Marian holdout who had built a formidable independent power base in Spain. The campaign was grueling. Pompey was defeated by Sertorius at the Battle of Lauron (76 BCE) and fought indecisive engagements in 75 BCE. Only after Sertorius was assassinated by his own lieutenant Perperna in 73/72 BCE could Pompey claim final victory. He burned Sertorius's correspondence unread — a gesture interpreted by some as statesmanlike, by others as political calculation.

### Spartacus and the Second Triumph (71 BCE)

Returning from Spain, Pompey intercepted and massacred approximately 6,000 fugitives from Crassus's decisive defeat of Spartacus — then claimed to have "ended the war." This deeply antagonized Crassus and was a source of lasting rivalry. Pompey nevertheless celebrated his second triumph.

### Pirates and Mithridates (67–62 BCE)

Two extraordinary laws transformed Pompey's power. The *Lex Gabinia* (67 BCE) granted him sweeping proconsular authority to combat Mediterranean piracy; he cleared the western Mediterranean in 40 days and completed the entire campaign in three months. The *Lex Manilia* (66 BCE), supported by Cicero's speech *Pro Lege Manilia*, added command of the Third Mithridatic War, replacing Lucullus — whom Pompey's detractors claimed he was merely cleaning up after.

In his eastern campaigns (66–62 BCE), Pompey defeated Mithridates VI of Pontus, invaded Armenia and received the submission of King Tigranes the Great, and annexed Syria as a Roman province (64 BCE). He redrew the political map of the eastern Mediterranean, establishing client kingdoms and provinces that would endure for centuries. Pompey claimed to have increased the annual state revenue from 200 million to 340 million sesterces. He celebrated his third triumph on his 45th birthday, 29 September 61 BCE.

## Political Career and the "First Triumvirate"

Pompey was first elected consul in 70 BCE alongside Crassus. Both were technically ineligible under Sulla's constitutional reforms, requiring a special senatorial decree. As consuls, they restored the powers of the plebeian tribune (including the veto) that Sulla had removed — widely seen as a turning point of the late Republic.

In 60 BCE, Pompey entered an informal political alliance with Julius Caesar and Marcus Licinius Crassus. This coalition — later termed the "First Triumvirate" by modern historiography — was not a formal constitutional office but a private arrangement among three powerful men. It was sealed by Pompey's marriage to Caesar's daughter Julia in 59 BCE. The alliance was renewed at the Luca Conference (56 BCE), but collapsed after Julia's death in childbirth (54 BCE) and Crassus's death at Carrhae (53 BCE). As the ancient historian Florus wrote, "Pompey could not brook an equal, or Caesar a superior."

In 52 BCE, after the murder of Publius Clodius Pulcher plunged Rome into violent chaos, Pompey was appointed sole consul — an extraordinary measure that concentrated immense power in his hands.

## Civil War and Death

When Caesar crossed the Rubicon in January 49 BCE, Pompey adopted a strategy of abandoning Italy, withdrawing his forces across the Adriatic to Dyrrhachium (modern Durrës, Albania). At the Battle of Dyrrhachium (late July 48 BCE), Pompey broke through Caesar's siege lines, forcing him to withdraw — the only time Caesar was defeated by Pompey in the civil war.

However, at the Battle of Pharsalus (9 August 48 BCE), under pressure from allied senators to give battle rather than continue his attrition strategy, Pompey was decisively defeated. His larger army (approximately 38,000 infantry, 7,000 cavalry) crumbled against Caesar's 22,000 infantry and 1,000 cavalry. Pharsalus was Pompey's only decisive battlefield defeat — but it was fatal.

Pompey fled to Egypt seeking refuge with the young king Ptolemy XIII. As he stepped ashore at Pelusium on 28 September 48 BCE (the eve of his 58th birthday), he was stabbed to death by Lucius Septimius, a Roman officer now serving in the Egyptian army, acting on the orders of Ptolemy's court. Ptolemy's advisors had calculated — wrongly — that killing Pompey would win Caesar's favor.

Pompey's body was cremated on the beach by two faithful servants. His head was kept briefly as evidence, then returned to his widow Cornelia Metella, who buried it at Pompey's villa in the Alban Hills. According to ancient sources, when Caesar was later presented with Pompey's severed head, he wept — an episode widely circulated but difficult to disentangle from Caesar's own propaganda and later literary embellishment.

## The Theatre of Pompey

Pompey's most enduring physical legacy was the Theatre of Pompey, Rome's first permanent stone theater, dedicated in 55 BCE. The complex included a vast colonnaded portico with gardens, fountains, and statues collected during his eastern campaigns, plus a curia (meeting hall). In a grim historical irony, it was in this Curia of Pompey that Julius Caesar was assassinated on 15 March 44 BCE — stabbed to death at the foot of a statue of his former ally and rival.

## Marriage and Family

Pompey was married five times: to Antistia (divorced at Sulla's order), Aemilia Scaura (Sulla's stepdaughter, died in childbirth), Mucia Tertia (mother of his three children — divorced in 61 BCE), Julia (Caesar's daughter — died in childbirth, 54 BCE), and Cornelia Metella (widowed by his death). His sons Gnaeus and Sextus continued the Pompeian cause after his death; Gnaeus was executed after the Battle of Munda (45 BCE), while Sextus maintained an independent state in Sicily until his execution in 35 BCE.

## Reception and Later Image

Pompey's posthumous image is complex and layered. Lucan's epic *Pharsalia* (1st century CE) portrayed him as a tragic hero, and later tradition often cast him as a "defender of the Republic" against Caesar's tyranny. This framing requires careful qualification: Pompey's entire career was built on bypassing Republican constitutional norms — commanding armies as a private citizen, holding extraordinary proconsular powers without having first held regular magistracies, and extracting unprecedented commands through popular legislation. His alignment with the *optimates* late in life was as much situational as ideological.

Pompey has been the subject of extensive dramatic and literary treatment: Shakespeare references him in *Julius Caesar* and *Antony and Cleopatra*; George Chapman (*The Wars of Pompey and Caesar*, c. 1604), Pierre Corneille (*La Mort de Pompée*, 1643), and John Masefield (*The Tragedy of Pompey the Great*, 1910) all wrote plays about him. Modern historical fiction by Colleen McCullough, Steven Saylor, and Robert Harris has extended his popular reception. These are creative works, not historical evidence.

A note on misattribution: the "Pompey's Pillar" in Alexandria, a 20.75-meter red granite column, has nothing to do with Pompey. It was erected in 298–302 CE in honor of Emperor Diocletian — some 350 years after Pompey's death.
