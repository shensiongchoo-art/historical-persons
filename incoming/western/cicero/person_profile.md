# Marcus Tullius Cicero — Biographical Profile

## Identity

Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BCE) was a Roman orator, statesman, lawyer, philosopher, and writer. Born in Arpinum to an equestrian family, he was a *novus homo* — the first in his family to reach the consulship.

## Consulship and the Catilinarian Conspiracy

Cicero's crowning political achievement was his consulship in 63 BCE, during which he uncovered and suppressed the Catilinarian conspiracy — a plot by the disaffected aristocrat Lucius Sergius Catilina to overthrow the Republic. Cicero ordered the execution of several conspirators without trial, citing the *senatus consultum ultimum*. This decision, while popular at the time, became a political liability: his enemies later used it to justify his exile in 58 BCE.

## Exile, Return, and Civil War

Cicero was recalled from exile in 57 BCE to popular acclaim. During the civil war between Caesar and Pompey, Cicero sided with Pompey — though reluctantly, and he had maintained relationships with both men. After Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus, Cicero accepted Caesar's clemency and returned to Rome, spending much of the dictatorship period writing philosophical works.

## Opposition to Antony and Death

After Caesar's assassination (44 BCE), Cicero emerged as a leading voice in the Senate and delivered the *Philippics* — fourteen speeches attacking Mark Antony. This opposition proved fatal. When Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate, Cicero was placed on the proscription list and was killed on 7 December 43 BCE.

## Literary and Philosophical Legacy

Cicero's surviving corpus is extraordinary: 58 speeches, over 900 letters, and numerous philosophical and rhetorical treatises. His writings are among the most important contemporary sources for the late Republic. His prose style became the model for Latin eloquence, influencing European literature and thought for two millennia.

## Source Caveats

Cicero's speeches are forensic advocacy, not neutral accounts. His letters, while more candid, reflect his own perspectives and biases. Later biographical traditions (Plutarch, Cassius Dio) add color but also rhetorical embellishment. The Fulvia hairpin story, while ancient, may be a dramatic invention rather than reliable history.
