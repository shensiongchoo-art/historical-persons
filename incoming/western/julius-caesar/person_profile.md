# Gaius Julius Caesar — Biographical Profile

## Identity

Gaius Julius Caesar (100–44 BCE) was a Roman general, statesman, and author. He was born into the patrician gens Julia, a family that claimed descent from the goddess Venus through Aeneas. His name later became the imperial title "Caesar" (and subsequently Kaiser, Tsar).

## Rise to Power

Caesar rose through the traditional Roman political ladder (cursus honorum), serving as quaestor, aedile, pontifex maximus, praetor, and finally consul in 59 BCE. In 60 BCE, he formed an informal political alliance with Pompey and Crassus — an arrangement later termed the "First Triumvirate" by historians, though it had no formal institutional status.

## Gallic Wars (58–50 BCE)

As proconsul of Gaul, Caesar conducted a series of campaigns that resulted in the conquest of all Gaul. He documented these campaigns in his *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, written in lucid third-person prose. This commentary is an invaluable primary source, though it should be read with awareness of Caesar's self-promotional aims.

## Civil War (49–45 BCE)

When the Senate, influenced by Pompey, ordered Caesar to disband his army, he crossed the Rubicon River in January 49 BCE — an act of insurrection that triggered civil war. He defeated Pompey at the Battle of Pharsalus (48 BCE), pursued him to Egypt (where Pompey was killed), and became involved in the Ptolemaic dynastic conflict, forming a relationship with Cleopatra VII. After further campaigns in Asia Minor, Africa, and Spain, he emerged as undisputed master of the Roman world.

## Dictatorship and Assassination

Caesar accumulated extraordinary powers, culminating in his appointment as *dictator perpetuo* (dictator for life) in February 44 BCE. On the Ides of March (15 March) 44 BCE, a group of approximately 60 senators led by Brutus and Cassius assassinated him in the Theatre of Pompey.

## Legacy

Caesar's posthumous adoption of his great-nephew Octavian ensured his political legacy. The name "Caesar" became synonymous with imperial authority. His calendar reform established the Julian calendar, which remained the standard in Europe until the Gregorian reform of 1582.

## Source Caveats

Caesar's own *Commentarii* are primary but self-interested. Plutarch's biography draws on earlier sources now lost but was written over a century later. Suetonius includes valuable biographical detail alongside anecdotal material. Cassius Dio provides a later synthesis. Cicero's contemporary letters offer a more immediate but partisan perspective from a political rival.
