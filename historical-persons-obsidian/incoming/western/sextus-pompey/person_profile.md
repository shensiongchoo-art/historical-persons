# Sextus Pompey (ca. 67–35 BCE)

## Identity Overview

Sextus Pompeius Magnus Pius (ca. 67–35 BCE) was a Roman commander of the late Republic, the younger son of Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (Pompey the Great). After his father's defeat and death in the civil war against Caesar, Sextus continued the Pompeian cause, first in Spain and later from a formidable naval power base in Sicily. From 42 to 36 BCE, he controlled the western Mediterranean sea lanes, threatened Rome's grain supply, and emerged as the last significant military obstacle to the Second Triumvirate's consolidation of power.

Ancient sources — all written under or after the victorious Augustan regime — systematically label Sextus a "pirate" (praedo in Latin). Modern scholarship recognizes this terminology as derogatory propaganda designed to delegitimize a political and military adversary. Sextus commanded a regular fleet, controlled territory, minted coins with his image, and presented himself as the legitimate heir of the Pompeian cause. This package uses "commander" or "naval commander" as neutral terms.

## Biography

### Early Life and the Civil War Against Caesar

Sextus was born around 67 BCE, the younger son of Pompey the Great and his third wife Mucia Tertia. His early life was spent in the shadow of his father's immense prestige. When civil war broke out between Caesar and Pompey in 49 BCE, Sextus was likely in Rome with his stepmother Cornelia Metella.

After Pompey's defeat at Pharsalus (48 BCE) and assassination in Egypt, Sextus and his elder brother Gnaeus Pompeius continued the Pompeian resistance in Africa and then in Spain. In 45 BCE, Caesar defeated the Pompeian forces at the Battle of Munda. Gnaeus was captured and executed; Sextus escaped and organized guerrilla resistance in Hispania.

### Rise to Power in Sicily

After Caesar's assassination (44 BCE), Sextus received an official command from the Senate to operate against Antony. When the Second Triumvirate was formed and the proscriptions began (43 BCE), Sextus used his fleet to rescue proscribed Romans and offer them sanctuary in Sicily. His forces swelled with refugees, and his naval power grew.

By 42 BCE, Sextus controlled Sicily, Sardinia, and Corsica. His fleet interdicted grain shipments to Rome, causing famine and civil unrest. The triumvirs were forced twice to negotiate with him: the Pact of Misenum (39 BCE) granted him control of Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, and the Peloponnese in exchange for lifting the grain blockade. The peace was short-lived.

### The War Against Octavian and Defeat at Naulochus

In 38 BCE, Octavian renewed hostilities against Sextus. The initial campaigns were disastrous for Octavian — Sextus's admirals, including Menas (Menodorus) and Demochares, repeatedly defeated Octavian's fleet.

In 36 BCE, Octavian's general Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa built a new fleet, trained crews in a purpose-built harbor (Portus Julius), and developed new naval tactics. Agrippa defeated Sextus's fleet at the Battle of Mylae, and then decisively at the Battle of Naulochus on 3 September 36 BCE. Sextus fled Sicily.

### Flight and Death

Sextus escaped to the East with a small squadron, seeking to negotiate with Mark Antony. He was captured at Miletus in 35 BCE by Marcus Titius, one of Antony's lieutenants, and executed without trial. The circumstances of his execution remain contested — whether Titius acted on Antony's orders or his own initiative is unclear in the sources.

## Source Problems

- **"Pirate" label**: The term praedo was systematically deployed by Augustan propaganda to deny Sextus political legitimacy. Modern scholarship (Hadas 1930, Powell and Welch 2002) demonstrates this was a deliberate discursive strategy.
- **No Pompeian sources survive**: All accounts come from the victors or from historians writing under the Augustan principate. Sextus's own perspective — his political program, his self-conception as pius (the Dutiful) — must be reconstructed from hostile sources and numismatic evidence.
- **Hostile narrative consistency**: Appian, Cassius Dio, and Velleius Paterculus all treat Sextus as a disruptive force. Plutarch's Life of Antony mentions Sextus only in passing. The unanimity of hostility suggests a propaganda line rather than independent historical judgment.
