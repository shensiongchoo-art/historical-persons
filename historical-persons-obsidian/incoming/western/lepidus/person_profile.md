# Marcus Aemilius Lepidus (ca. 89–13 BCE)

## Identity Overview

Marcus Aemilius Lepidus (ca. 89–13 BCE) was a Roman politician of the late Republic. He served as praetor (49 BCE), magister equitum (Master of Horse) under Julius Caesar (46–44 BCE), consul (46 BCE), and a member of the Second Triumvirate alongside Mark Antony and Octavian (43–36 BCE). He held the office of pontifex maximus (chief priest) from 44 BCE until his death in 13 BCE.

Lepidus is the least documented of the three triumvirs. His reputation in both ancient and modern sources tends toward that of a marginal figure — the "weakest" triumvir. This framing needs careful treatment: many surviving ancient accounts were written under or after Augustus and reflect pro-Augustan bias against Lepidus as an obstacle to Octavian's rise.

## Biography

### Early Career

Lepidus was born into the patrician gens Aemilia, one of Rome's oldest families. His father, Marcus Aemilius Lepidus (consul 78 BCE), led a failed revolt against the Sullan constitution and died in Sardinia. Little is known of the younger Lepidus's early life.

He enters the historical record as praetor in 49 BCE, the year Caesar crossed the Rubicon. Lepidus apparently sided with Caesar during the civil war. In 46 BCE, Caesar appointed him magister equitum (Master of Horse), a position tantamount to Caesar's deputy during his dictatorship. That same year, Lepidus served as consul alongside Caesar.

After Caesar's assassination (44 BCE), Lepidus commanded troops near Rome as governor of Gallia Narbonensis and Hispania Citerior. In the power vacuum following the assassination, he initially proposed avenging Caesar but was persuaded by Mark Antony to seek a political settlement. He became pontifex maximus in 44 BCE, succeeding the murdered Caesar.

### The Second Triumvirate

In November 43 BCE, Lepidus joined Antony and Octavian in forming the Second Triumvirate (triumviri rei publicae constituendae) — a legally constituted commission with extraordinary powers for five years. The three divided the Roman world: Octavian received the West, Antony the East, and Lepidus the Spanish provinces and Gallia Narbonensis. The proscriptions that followed claimed the lives of many Roman elites, including Cicero.

Lepidus's role was consistently secondary. In 42 BCE, while Antony and Octavian campaigned against Caesar's assassins at Philippi, Lepidus governed Italy. After Philippi, his influence waned — his colleagues redistributed provinces, reducing Lepidus to the governance of Africa, which he held from 40 to 36 BCE.

### Marginalization and Later Life

In 36 BCE, during Octavian's war against Sextus Pompey, Lepidus attempted to assert control over Sicily with his African legions after Sextus's defeat. Octavian confronted Lepidus's troops, who deserted en masse. Octavian stripped Lepidus of his triumviral powers and provinces but allowed him to retain the office of pontifex maximus.

Lepidus spent the remaining 23 years of his life in forced political retirement, living at Circeii (or Circaeum) on the Italian coast. When he died in 13 BCE, Augustus himself assumed the pontificate — a symbolic transfer that closed the last remnant of the triumviral era.

## Source Problems

Lepidus presents significant source challenges:

- **Thin ancient coverage**: Unlike Caesar, Antony, or Octavian, Lepidus has no dedicated biography by Plutarch or Suetonius. His actions are reconstructed from scattered references in Appian, Cassius Dio, and Cicero's letters.
- **Pro-Augustan bias**: Accounts of the conflict over Sicily (36 BCE) invariably portray Lepidus as incompetent or presumptuous. This framing serves the Augustan narrative of Octavian as the rightful consolidator of power.
- **Weakness trope**: The characterization of Lepidus as "weak" or "the forgotten triumvir" is partly reception and partly a reflection of his genuine political marginalization. The two are difficult to disentangle.
