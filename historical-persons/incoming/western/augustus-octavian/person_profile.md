# Augustus (Gaius Octavius / Octavian / Augustus) — Biographical Profile

## Name and Identity

Augustus was born **Gaius Octavius** on 23 September 63 BCE in Rome, on the Palatine Hill. His father, also Gaius Octavius, was a praetor and proconsular governor of Macedonia who died when the future Augustus was four years old. His mother, Atia Balba Caesonia, was the niece of Julius Caesar — a family connection that would prove decisive.

According to Suetonius (*Life of Augustus* 7.1), the infant Octavius received the cognomen **Thurinus**, commemorating his father's victory over the remnants of Spartacus's followers at Thurii. Cassius Dio (45.1.1) gives a different name, Caepias, likely a corruption of his birth tribe Scaptia.

Julius Caesar, lacking a legitimate son, deposited his will with the Vestal Virgins in 45 BCE, naming the young Octavius as his principal heir on condition that he assume Caesar's name. After Caesar's assassination on 15 March 44 BCE, Octavius accepted the will on 8 May and became **Gaius Julius Caesar**. Contemporaries, including Cicero, appended "Octavianus" to distinguish him from Caesar. Modern historians conventionally use the name **Octavian** for the period between 44 and 27 BCE.

The legal status of this testamentary adoption was ambiguous — full adoption normally required a *lex curiata* during the father's lifetime. Octavian had this law passed only in August 43 BCE, after he had marched on Rome and become consul at age 19.

On 16 January 27 BCE, the Senate granted him the honorific title **Augustus** ("the revered one"), proposed by Munatius Plancus. His full style became **Imperator Caesar Divi Filius Augustus** — "Commander Caesar, son of the deified one, the Revered." He died on 19 August 14 CE at Nola, aged 75, and was deified by the Senate on 17 September.

## The Rise to Power

### The Second Triumvirate (43–36 BCE)

Following Caesar's assassination, the 19-year-old Octavian maneuvered between rivals and allies with remarkable political skill. He initially allied with Cicero against Mark Antony, then — after consolidating his position — joined Antony and Lepidus to form the **Second Triumvirate** in October 43 BCE at Bononia. The alliance was formalized by the *Lex Titia* on 27 November 43 BCE.

The Triumvirate immediately initiated proscriptions — the systematic execution of political enemies — which claimed Cicero on 7 December 43 BCE. The following year, at the Battle of Philippi (October 42 BCE), the armies of the Triumvirate defeated Brutus and Cassius, the leading assassins of Caesar, who both committed suicide.

On 1 January 42 BCE, the Senate deified Julius Caesar as *Divus Iulius*. Octavian now styled himself *Divi filius* — "son of the divine" — a politically potent title that distinguished him from all rivals.

The Triumvirate divided the Roman world: Octavian took the West, Antony the East, and Lepidus received Africa. In 36 BCE, Agrippa — Octavian's lifelong friend and most capable general — defeated Sextus Pompeius at the naval Battle of Naulochus. When Lepidus attempted to claim Sicily for himself, his troops deserted to Octavian, and Lepidus was removed from power, allowed to live in quiet retirement though retaining the office of Pontifex Maximus.

### Actium and the Conquest of Egypt (32–30 BCE)

The alliance with Mark Antony unraveled as Antony transferred his loyalties to Cleopatra VII of Egypt. In 32 BCE, Antony divorced Octavian's sister Octavia. Octavian seized Antony's will from the Temple of Vesta, revealing that Antony planned to be buried in Alexandria and had made generous provisions for his children by Cleopatra. The Senate declared war — on Cleopatra, not Antony, a calculated propaganda move framing the conflict as a foreign war rather than civil war.

On 2 September 31 BCE, Agrippa's fleet decisively defeated Antony and Cleopatra at the **Battle of Actium**. The following year, Octavian entered Alexandria. Antony and Cleopatra committed suicide, and Caesarion — the son of Julius Caesar and Cleopatra — was executed on Octavian's orders. Egypt was annexed as Octavian's personal province, providing him with enormous wealth and a secure grain supply for Rome.

## The Constitutional Settlements

### The First Settlement (27 BCE)

On 13 January 27 BCE, Octavian staged a carefully choreographed "restoration of the Republic," returning his extraordinary powers to the Senate and people. The Senate responded by granting him the title **Augustus** and entrusting him with a 10-year command of provinces considered unstable or threatened — which happened to contain approximately 20 of the roughly 25 legions.

The *Res Gestae* (chapter 34), composed by Augustus himself, presents this moment as: "I transferred the republic from my power to the dominion of the senate and people of Rome." This is Augustus's own narrative — it is not neutral history. Modern scholars view the settlement as political theater that established de facto autocracy under republican institutional forms.

### The Second Settlement (23 BCE)

A serious illness in 23 BCE forced Augustus to confront the question of succession. He resigned the annual consulship he had held continuously since 31 BCE and instead received two powers that would define the Principate:

- **Tribunicia potestas** (tribunician power) for life — the authority to convene the Senate, propose legislation, and veto any public action
- **Imperium proconsulare maius** (greater proconsular power) — authority over all provincial governors, applicable throughout the empire

These powers, combined with his personal *auctoritas* (influence, prestige), formed the constitutional basis of Augustan rule. He did not hold a single office called "emperor" — his power derived from a bundle of specific powers held simultaneously, an unprecedented concentration of authority in Roman constitutional practice.

### Later Honors

In 12 BCE, after the death of Lepidus, Augustus became **Pontifex Maximus** (chief priest), uniting supreme religious authority with supreme political power. In 2 BCE, the Senate granted him the title **Pater Patriae** (Father of the Country).

## The Res Gestae

Augustus composed the *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* (The Achievements of the Divine Augustus), a first-person funerary inscription in 35 chapters originally engraved on bronze pillars outside his Mausoleum in Rome. The most complete surviving copy is the **Monumentum Ancyranum** in Ankara, Turkey, inscribed in both Latin and Greek.

The text narrates his military achievements (claiming 21 acclamations as *imperator*), benefactions to the Roman people, and building program (claiming restoration of 82 temples in 28 BCE alone). It emphasizes *auctoritas* over *potestas*, presenting his power as flowing from personal prestige rather than formal office. Significantly, the *Res Gestae* omits the Teutoburg Forest disaster of 9 CE and presents the proscriptions in euphemistic language.

## Key Relationships

**Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa**: Lifelong friend, most capable general, and architect of victory at Actium. Married Augustus's daughter Julia in 21 BCE. Granted tribunician power in 18 BCE — effectively co-regent. Died 12 BCE.

**Livia Drusilla**: Wife from 38 BCE for 51 years until his death. Already pregnant with her second son Drusus at the time of their marriage. Powerful political advisor; rumors of her poisoning Augustus (reported by Tacitus and Cassius Dio) are rejected by most modern historians.

**Julia the Elder**: Augustus's only biological child (by Scribonia, born 39 BCE). Married successively to Marcellus, Agrippa, and Tiberius. Exiled in 2 BCE for adultery — a scandal that exposed tensions in Augustus's moral legislation and dynastic plans.

**Tiberius**: Livia's son by her first marriage. Adopted as heir in 4 CE after the deaths of Augustus's grandsons Gaius and Lucius Caesar. Succeeded Augustus as princeps in 14 CE.

## Physical Appearance and Portraiture

Suetonius (*Life of Augustus* 79) describes Augustus as unusually handsome, with clear bright eyes and light brown, slightly curly hair. He stood about 5 feet 9 inches (approximately 1.7 meters), short by Roman standards.

His official portrait statues — most famously the **Augustus of Prima Porta** (Vatican Museums) — depict him as perpetually youthful and idealized. From 27 BCE until his death at 75, he is consistently shown as a young man in his prime, a calculated departure from the realistic, age-depicting veristic portraiture of the late Republic. The Prima Porta statue's breastplate depicts the return of the Parthian standards (20 BCE), and the small Cupid at his foot alludes to the Julian family's claimed descent from Venus.

The **Via Labicana Augustus** (Museo Nazionale Romano) shows him veiled as Pontifex Maximus — the pious priest rather than the military commander. Together, these images represent complementary facets of Augustan propaganda: divine descent, military victory, and religious piety.

## Historiographical Assessment

The central debate in Augustan historiography — articulated most influentially by Ronald Syme in *The Roman Revolution* (1939) — asks whether Augustus was a restorer of the Republic or the creator of a monarchical system. Modern scholarly consensus, represented by biographers such as Adrian Goldsworthy (2014) and Pat Southern (2014), holds that Augustus ruled as a de facto autocrat whose power ultimately rested on military control, while maintaining the facade of republican institutions. The Principate was not a pre-planned design but evolved through trial, error, and improvisation.

Augustus's military record presents a sharp contrast with his political genius. Modern consensus holds that he was a poor-to-mediocre field commander — bedridden during the first battle at Philippi, and dependent on Agrippa, Tiberius, Drusus, and Germanicus for his military victories. His true talents lay in political maneuvering, propaganda, and the art of delegation.

The term "first Roman emperor" is a modern historiographical category, not an ancient constitutional title. Augustus held no office called *imperator* in the sense of "emperor." He was *princeps* — "first citizen" — and his system, retrospectively named the Principate, maintained the outward forms of the Roman Republic while concentrating effective power in a single individual for life.
